# The Creative Strategist Playbook
**Source:** 8-Week Mentorship Cohort · **Coaches:** Luke, Ryan, Cosmin · **Added:** 2026-06-21
**Format:** 17 lessons pulled from real coaching calls, Slack threads, and homework across one eight-week cohort. Read it as a playbook, not a transcript.

## The Moment We're In

Being a great creative strategist is the closest thing to free money in the marketing world. Demand wildly outstrips the supply of people who are actually good at it. One operator studied this cohort — a founder running a brand that did roughly $75M last year chasing $250M — said plainly they'd pay $50,000 a month for the right CS.

Members of this cohort closed deals carrying $10,000 performance bonuses, 3–5% of $2,000,000/month in ad spend, and rates on track for multiple six figures a year.

### Cohort Results

| Metric | Amount |
|---|---|
| Combined starting MRR | ~$49,500/mo |
| Combined current MRR | ~$73,300/mo |
| Combined peak MRR | ~$86,800/mo |
| Net MRR added (current) | ~$23,800/mo |
| Net MRR added (peak) | ~$37,300/mo |
| Cohort goal | ~$110,000/mo |

**ROI math:** Tuition was $3,500. The new income added in month one alone (~$23,800/mo) already paid the whole cohort back — roughly a 1.13× return inside the first month. Annualized on current numbers: ~$285,600 added income, ~13.6× return; peak basis ~$447,600, ~21.3×.

**Average member:** went from ~$8,250/mo to ~$12,217/mo — a jump of ~$3,967/mo (+48%), or ~$6,217/mo (+75%) at peak.

**The headline finding: mindset drove the money more than skill did.** The two cleanest one-week wins of the entire cohort were both internal shifts, not tactics.

### Member Arcs

**Member 1 — Unlocked speed**
Start: ~$17–18k/mo. A perfectionist who never felt finished. Reached $30k/month plus performance bonuses inside eight weeks — clearing both their 12-month and 3-year goals at once. The whole unlock was speed: learning to ship at the right altitude and stop over-polishing work nobody was asking them to over-polish.

**Member 2 — Working less, charging more**
Start: ~$12k/mo. Landed a blue-chip client ramping to $15k, putting them on a ~$24–25k trajectory — and did it while firing a $5k client.

**Member 3 — The comeback**
Start: $6,500/mo. Arc: $6.5k → raise from $2k to $5k on one client in Week 2 → resigning an exploitative client → ~$11.5k base by Weeks 6–7, plus a new deal paying 3–5% of ad spend on a brand spending $2M/month.

**Member 4 — From zero, longest runway**
Start: $0. Went $0 → spec client → two paying clients by Week 3 → peak ~$10,400/mo.

**Member 5 — From zero, the self-catch**
Start: $0. Zero for five weeks, then two clients signed in Week 6 (~$2,000 base plus ad-spend percentages). Standout moment: asked to drop outreach from eight to five a day, then eight hours later wrote "jk, I locked in and did it" — catching their own backslide in real time.

**Member 6 — Strong pipeline, no close yet**
Start: ~$5–13.5k (variable). Ended pipeline-heavy with no new client closed — the cautionary tale. Real progress: raising rate floor from $75 to $150/ad and $500 to $1,000/advertorial.

## Lesson 1 — Common Mental Blocks

The thing standing between you and the income you want is almost never skill. The bottleneck was, over and over, a mental block wearing a tactical disguise.

### The Five Reasons People Fail (Luke's Framework)

1. **You're not good.** "You just suck — surprisingly, the number one reason." You are the product; if the product is weak, nothing else matters.
2. **You suck to work with.** Missed deadlines, poor communication, blind spots nobody's ever called you out on.
3. **You're scared — a people-pleaser.** You don't ask for referrals, raises, or bigger clients. You undersell yourself, have no boundaries, let people take advantage of you. Clients love people like this. It just sucks for you.
4. **You're egotistical and unaware.** Not coachable.
5. **You're inconsistent and entitled, with the wrong expectations.** The 10X Rule: "whatever you think the amount of work is, it's going to be ten times that."

Number three is the dominant block in almost every cohort. The good news: it's the most fixable, because the fix is mostly courage reps.

### Recurring Patterns

**Control-as-safety / compulsive over-delivery** — The high performer's disease. A refusal to share a draft below 90%. The underlying fear is that any mistake is catastrophic, so the instinct is to try to engineer the possibility of failure out of existence entirely. "There's really no safety except in surrender."

**The over-engineer** — Complexity as armor. The belief that you need an elaborate system in place before you're allowed to act. "You've put 1,600 steps between you and what you want." The healthy version: do the simplest, most obvious version of the thing today, and let action teach you.

**Glossing your own wins** — One member glossed straight past an $824,000 advertorial like it was nothing. "You talk down your own work because you don't believe you're good enough" — with zero evidence, since no one had ever actually told them their skills were mediocre.

**"I have to earn my worth" (the people-pleaser)** — Deliver first, then maybe deserve to ask. One member won a raise, then voluntarily piled on free work to "justify" it, climbing back into a slightly larger version of the cage they'd just escaped. The fix: let the ask come first, on its own merits.

**Self-erasure via speed** — Talking fast and making yourself small to keep everyone comfortable. Often pairs with a punishing cycle: committing to high numbers, missing them, and "taking a little more away from my own confidence" each time.

### Smaller Blocks Worth Knowing

- **Hedge language is asking for permission.** Watch for "preferably," "I guess," "kind of," "maybe."
- **Downplaying your wins keeps you stuck.** It leaks straight into how you ask.
- **"Getting better" can be procrastination.** The skill mostly comes from working with more brands, not more solo studying.
- **Treating opportunity as a chore.** A near-dream inbound felt like "an inconvenience — another thing I have to do."
- **The fallback-job hedge.** "You're hanging on to this job because of the belief that this won't work."
- **"I don't know" usually means "I'm not willing to look."**
- **The Box of Beliefs** — you hold a belief, go hunting for evidence, find it, and now get to be right about it forever.

## Lesson 2 — The Most Important Mindset Things

Three pillars: keeping your word, your ways of being (the alter ego), and trust over fear.

"All transformation comes down to two things — you have to be aware, and you have to make a different choice. That's it."

### 2a · Being Your Word

This is the single most-repeated idea in the entire mentorship: "this whole thing boils down to being your word — everything else falls into place."

**The definition:** Being your word means you do what you said you'd do, and if something's going to get in the way, you tell the other person in advance. Vague commitments build no trust — "might have this for you next week" means nothing; "you'll have it Friday at 3pm Eastern" means everything.

**The reframe that lands hardest: your word is your self-esteem.**
> Every time you break your word to yourself, you're withdrawing self-esteem — you're proving "I'm not someone who's a person of my word." That weird, icky feeling when you procrastinate? That's the deposit of self-esteem leaving. It's not about the agreement. It's about you.

**The renegotiation rule:** A renegotiation only counts if you do it before you've blown the deadline, while you can still hit the original commitment. An hour-before "can I get another day" isn't a renegotiation — that's a broken word with a bow on it.

**Supporting frames:**
- Make commitments binary, not "I'll try." "I commit to having one client by next Friday" is something you'll clearly hit or miss. "I'll try" is hedge language in a costume.
- Word equals self-worth: when a member kept skipping homework, the coaching was "the belief is the homework isn't important — which decodes to my word isn't important."
- Word equals referrals equals money: Luke knows talented people missing six figures of work he'd happily refer — except they can't be trusted to keep their word.

### 2b · Risks, Courage, and Found Money

**"Found money"** is the fastest ROI in this business. One strategist realized they were badly undercharging, asked for a 100% raise, and instantly added $24,000 a year from one conversation.

Across the cohort, found money came from: renegotiating what you already have, dropping bad clients to free capacity, saying no to more in order to protect proof you're building, and trading up.

**On courage:**
- Courage is the metric mid-transition — and your number can go down. "In numbers you went down, but the courage it took is huge."
- Facing the fear is the unlock. "My entire jump came down to one thing: facing my fear of taking on more work."
- Going 100% means being seen messy. "It means you'll fail, you'll be messy, you'll step on toes, and you'll be held responsible."
- Your life is now. Used on the member deferring a launch to Q4 ("why not now?").

### 2c · The Alter Ego (Ways of Being)

The premise: you don't need to learn confidence — you need to turn it on.

**The method:**
1. Pick the trait you lack and find a character — real or fictional — who fully embodies it (must be a genuine other, not a slightly better version of you)
2. Name it, and write 5–10 "ways of being" words
3. Anchor it in your body
4. Turn it on in the exact moments you'd normally default to the old pattern

**Cohort alter egos:**
- The "stupid" one — antidote to over-engineering. Rule: when you catch yourself optimizing, do it the laziest, most obvious, most textbook way.
- The confident ad-man — for the member who couldn't access unearned confidence. "He would just say what he wants to say."
- The fierce dealmaker — humor, edge, "figure it out later," makes deals, takes up space. Words: audacious, courageous, confident, direct, free, abundant.
- The free spirit — for the over-controller: adventure, fun, savoring the moment.

**Highest-leverage application: put the alter ego into your copy, not just your outreach.** "Tap into the alter ego when you write. I'd rather you be too obnoxious and have to dial it back than be timid and have to ramp it up."

## Lesson 3 — Rapid-Fire Mindset Hacks

Portable reframes you pull out in the moment when you feel yourself spinning.

### The Founding Distinctions
- **Feedback is data, not attack.** It's one person's experience of you — it doesn't mean it's true.
- **Protection versus discovery.** Protection is defending, telling a story, laughing it off. Discovery is letting it sit and asking: maybe it's true, maybe not — but I want to know.
- **Facts versus interpretation.** A client not replying for three days is a fact; everything your brain adds is a screenplay.
- **How you do anything is how you do everything.** Your character pattern and your copy pattern are usually the same pattern.

### On Power, Story, and Accountability
- "As soon as you default to story and excuse, you give away your power."
- Victim versus accountability: stuck in victim, everything gets worse; the moment you're accountable, you take your power back.
- Responsibility isn't blame. Blame is "it's my fault." Responsibility is "it's up to me to make it happen."
- **Language audit:** "I have to" is a necessity; "it's in my nature" is a permanent identity — and if the reason you do something is a permanent identity, there's nothing anyone can do for you.

### On Confidence and Self-Worth
- "You don't get to be a top creative strategist and believe you're dogshit. Pick which one you're more committed to."
- "If you reek of insecurity, people won't work with you" — they feel it even over text.
- **Receive the win** — nothing to prove or qualify, just receive it.
- "Would you say that to a child? No? But you say it to yourself all day."

### On Speed, Simplicity, and Pressure
- "To do more in less time, give yourself less time — and you'll figure it out."
- "If you were stupid, how would you do this?" — the over-thinker bypass.
- "Try" is banned. "Define your time and you'll make it happen."

### On Feeling and the Body
- "You don't think a feeling, you feel it — in your body, not vocalized."
- Experience the emotion; don't act out of it. Feel it, let it pass through, then choose.
- "I am experiencing ___" — the core ritual: name your thoughts, body sensations, and emotions in real time, no qualifiers.

### On Where to Look and How to Choose
- Your growth edge is where the resistance is.
- The billion-dollar test: if you'd get a billion for making the call by Friday and lose an arm if you didn't, would you have made it? Then it wasn't "couldn't," it was "didn't."
- "A maybe is a no. If it's not a heck yes, it's a no."
- "It's not up to the client whether it was good — it's up to the market."

## Lesson 4 — The Simplest Client-Getting Formula

**Get proof, and do consistent outreach.** That's literally it.

Everything else is just detail on those two levers. Ryan's version: "be your word, do your outreach, and the rest will come."

### Lever One: Proof Determines Your Income

"Your proof is going to determine the income you get. I wish it was your skill, but it's the proof. Skill helps you get more proof — but proof is the number one way to keep getting clients."

**The proof ladder:**
Resume → Samples → Certifications → Testimonials → Track record (ad spend / ROAS) → Case studies → Blue-chip clients

The fastest way up the middle rungs is to bootstrap them. The fastest route to track record specifically is agencies.

### Lever Two: Consistent Outreach

A written commitment, hit daily or weekly. As one member reminded themselves: "I must not stop outreach forever just because I hit my income goal."

### 4a · Blue-Chip Clients

Most people assume the biggest, best-known brands are the hardest to land. The coaches argue the opposite — and the cohort proved it.

**They're often your best clients.** Blue-chip brands understand how this works — they know not everything's a winner, and they're easy to work with because they run real systems instead of chaos. 98% of people assume they can't get them and never apply.

**They hire on skill and curiosity, not your numbers.** One member landed a coveted blue-chip role and the copy chief barely cared about their big past ad — they wanted to know their copywriting inspirations and whether they were a curious person who loved to learn ("have you read Breakthrough Advertising? What do you know about market sophistication and awareness?"). **Reading the foundational books is literally what gets you in.**

**They arrive inbound once your reputation exists.** Two members landed blue-chip work this cohort with no cold outreach — one from a LinkedIn inbound, one from a referral.

**Two traps to avoid:** The proof-first reality (newer strategists need to work smaller brands first specifically to get ad spend behind their ads), and the validation trap — wanting a dream brand to want you so you can be the one to turn them down. That's ego, not strategy.

**What counts as proof?** All of it: creative-strategy work, advertorials, emails, sales pages. Stack creative-strategy work high in your portfolio, then everything else underneath. Testimonials: send every past client a draft they can edit, aim for five — but praise screenshots are usable proof in the meantime.

## Lesson 5 — Overall Outreach Principles

Most strategists fail at outreach not because they can't write a message, but because they treat outreach as the last thing they do instead of the first, and because their energy is "please pick me" instead of "let's see if you're a fit."

**Core principles:**
- **Consistency is the unlock.** Two responses from a week of steady outreach was proof enough for one member that the only missing ingredient had been consistency.
- **Lead with a concrete result.** One member leads every message with a past brand they took from $100k to $350k a month, and gets a reply roughly one in five times.
- **Position from abundance, and don't ask permission to start.** Cosmin doesn't even ask if they want to work together — he assumes it: "I'm excited to get started, let me know when."
- **Don't compare yourself, and don't ask how you stacked up against other candidates** — it puts you in a position of weakness instantly.
- **Match the method to your proof.** When your proof is elite, you don't need an elaborate machine, just volume.
- **Follow-up beats timing.** Ryan once reached out to a nine-figure brand, heard nothing, and got a "sorry, this got lost, we'd love to work with you" a full year later. There is no "wait until I'm better." Apply now.
- **The block is usually beingness, not the message.** When a member's strong outreach kept getting ignored, the coaches looked at the messages, found nothing wrong, and pointed back at confidence.
- **Do the high-effort applications, not just the easy ones** — the in-depth applications most people skip are where the good gigs are.

**Business truths underneath all of it:** Clients only care about making money, so lead with money made. Performance pays far more than base. You only create what you commit to. Abundance keeps everything neutral — "if it doesn't work, it doesn't work; there's no emotion, just here's my rate."

### Cosmin's Outreach SOP

**1. The mindset: you have what it takes.** Don't walk in hoping to get accepted — walk in knowing you're a fit. If they don't move forward, it's either something they don't see yet or it's not the right fit.

**2. On the call: process first, audit back.** Don't sell results — talk about your process and what you've done, casually dropping proof. Then audit them: what's the rate, what are the deliverables, what are your expectations? You're deciding too.

**3. Frame everything positively.** "Are you available?" becomes "Yes — I'm reachable for anything urgent, and I also block time for deep work, because that's where my best output comes from." Transparency builds trust faster than dodging.

**4. Promise the process, never results.** "I can't promise any single ad will win. I can promise I'll keep cracking until one does."

**5. Volume kills scarcity.** Scarcity gets smelled on every call. The fix isn't journaling — it's pipeline. Run enough outreach that losing any client is a shrug, not a crisis.

**6. Quick hits:** Ignore "3+ years required" — don't address it, just apply. Skip the recruiter on scope; get on the call with the brand. Apply to blue-chip now. Speed matters — apply fast after a post goes up. Don't replay the weird calls — bombed, ghosted, brushed off, it's usually them, not you. Next outreach.

### Pre-Send Checklist

- Volume is non-negotiable (outreach is the first thing you do, not the last)
- Speed beats perfection (10 minutes, not 45)
- Use the nuclear VSL (a 2–3 minute Loom, 80% templated)
- Make it obvious it isn't a mass-send (their brand name, a specific ad)
- Know your price before you reach out
- Don't over-explain (shorter than a phone screen)
- Follow up, then follow up again (a 2-day cadence — most people quit after one message)
- Use spec work as a weapon, not a hiding place
- Stop limiting yourself with arbitrary rules before they've even said yes
- Use every channel

## Lesson 6 — Negotiation & Pricing Tactics

Negotiation is almost entirely a function of self-worth, not tactics. The number you charge is a readout of what you believe you're worth.

### Set the Floor Before the Test, Hold It Through the Counters

Cosmin's signature story: a brand offers "five test ads for $300." He says sure, he'll basically do the test for free — but his rate going forward is $250/ad. They get a winner and counter ("how about $150/hr?"). He says no. They keep negotiating. He ends up over a $10k retainer.

**The rule:** set your boundaries before the test, not after, because it's far harder after. Keep "getting in the door" and "what I charge" as two separate conversations.

### Core Mechanics

- **Make them name the number first.** "Whoever names the number first loses information." When they say "$1–3M/month," you know what to charge.
- **Ignore the "requirements" box.** Most of the time the client doesn't actually know — all they want is better ads and more spend.
- **Price by channel.** Agencies cap you around $75–100/ad because they take a cut. Private clients should be priced high — quoting $75 to a private client is itself a credibility hit.
- **The per-ad grid:** $50 / $75 / $100 / $150 / $250 per ad. Your number times realistic weekly volume equals the client's value to you. ("$250/ad × 100/week is $50–100k a month.")
- **"Full-time" is meaningless — price deliverables.** Reframe as the client's advantage: "yes I'm available, but if you interrupt me every hour you're taking away the deep work."
- **Set a stretchy floor and hold it.** "If it feels comfortable, you're not charging enough."
- **Apply your floor to existing clients too.** A floor you only use on strangers isn't a floor. Your best client is often the one most overdue for a raise.
- **Don't pre-concede.** "It's amazing you were going to lower prices when no one said anything about it."

### Performance Is Where the Real Money Is

On a brand spending $1M+/month, 2.5–5% of a scaling ad is thousands a month from a single creative. Sequence it: get in, win, then renegotiate from leverage.

### The Predatory-NDA Lesson

Checklist when reviewing an NDA: does it conflict with a client you already have, is the scope narrow (trade secrets only, not your whole category), and are the damages and the time-tail reasonable?

**The posture lesson:** a draft that tries to find "a way that doesn't strangle me" is still asking permission to breathe. The move is to set your own terms and let them decide.

### Worked Deal Structures (Reference Menu)

- New clients: ~$5k base + 2.5–5% ad spend
- Agency: $100/ad + 5% if 10%+ are winners
- New brand: $250/ad + performance
- 9-figure brand: $7.5k ramping to $15k + 2% ad spend
- One client raised from $2k to $5k; referral brand at $3k base + 3–5% of ad spend at 1.5 ROAS+
- Trial at $3k base + 1% on spend over $1k + $2k bonus at $100k spend
- Full worked example: "$1,500/month flat + 3% ad-spend fee for creatives above 1.3 ROAS"

### Live Role-Play Arc (Collapse to Control)

- **The collapse:** "Let's start at $50/ad, since you're a newer brand."
- **After anchoring confidence:** "We're going to start at $150 per ad — I know the ads I'm going to run, we'll have lots to test, and we'll build that brand up."
- **Concede to a floor with a trigger:** "At most $100/ad — and once I get you three winners, we go to $150."
- **Walk away from abundance:** "If we can't do $100, I'll connect you with someone in my network at a lower rate — but that's my minimum to start."

"Newer brand" is the client's frame, not your ceiling. Anchor high, hold, refer out from abundance.

### Worked Thread — Restructuring a "Full-Time" Arrangement

Long, careful email asking to move from on-call full-time to contractor deal. Cosmin's feedback: "you're over-explaining and giving them an out. If they want you, they'll find a solution."

**The rewrite:** short and confident — "I've been thinking about a new structure that'll help me do my job better and get you better results while being more efficient with my time, a win-win long-term; I'll explain on the call." Frame everything positively. The long, values-heavy version gives them an out; the short, win-framed version assumes the relationship continues and lets you deliver the real content live, from strength.

## Lesson 7 — Specific Tactics: Upwork, X, Hiry, LinkedIn

### Upwork

Bootstrap reviews — have people in your network hire you for $5, $10, $50 and fund it yourself (costs about $3 to start). You need at least five reviews to look credible. Get the profile to 100% with a video, list specific deliverables (VSL, statics, advertorials — not "direct response ad creatives"), set rate at $75–100/hr, add at least three portfolio pieces. Check Upwork about three times a day.

**The lesson in one line:** lead with proof, keep it shorter than a phone screen, end with a clear next step, and answer "why should I care?" from their side.

### X / Twitter

Search "hiring copywriter" or "hiring creative strategist," filter to the last day or week, comment on the post and check DMs, then message leading with a specific result and proposing a call (alternate close: a free three-pack of ads in 48 hours). Reply rate is about one in five.

### Hiry

A vetted talent network — get "on record" so clients come looking for you. The video portion matters, and inbound replies count.

**Hiry lesson:** when you have a recognized brand and a real number, name-drop and stop selling; raise the rate as a follow-up, never the lead.

### LinkedIn

Optimize the profile (a headline naming the kind of brands you serve, an About section on how you see marketing and what makes you different, a Featured section with spec or video, and three to five posts). **Handwritten content beats AI for reach.** One member started networking on a Tuesday and had an interview booked by Thursday from only six connections. One member's $250/ad deal came straight off a LinkedIn inbound.

### The Nuclear VSL

A 2–3 minute Loom with slides — a proof-compensator for when your track record is thin. Mostly templated; you only personalize the open and the "here's what I'd do for you" section. Use it selectively. Coach feedback: add brands you've written for, and introduce yourself before the ask.

### Keeping the Pipeline Hot

Always be outreaching, even with a full pipeline. A simple weekly system: pick one or all three of — post on LinkedIn, talk to three people in the space (strategists, media buyers, editors), and add people on LinkedIn. Pause at capacity if you must, but never let the muscle die.

## Lesson 8 — Responding & Following Up With Clients

Most deals aren't lost on the pitch — they're lost in the silence after it.

### The Follow-Up Cadence

Deliver Monday → follow up Tuesday evening or Wednesday in their timezone → give a day or two → propose a call → follow up again Friday → if still silent, again in a week or two. Follow up in the client's waking hours across time zones.

### Inbound Is Warm — Bring Abundance, Not Chore-Energy

The abundance posture is itself the close. When inbound comes through a recruiter, pass the ball back rather than making an offer: "I'm interested in these brands — can you tell me more about their offer?"

### Stay in Communication, Set Boundaries Cleanly

Don't go quiet and surface only at the deadline. When a trial comes back with feedback, tell rather than ask ("I'll have the revision back in two days," not "is that okay?"). Use fresh wins as a reason to follow up. As Ryan put it: "they'll respond to whatever kind of person you are; if you get them results, none of the rest matters."

### Worked Thread — Setting Up a Renegotiation Call

Soft draft: "you got a sec this week? Wanted to do a quick catch-up… I'd like to revisit the retainer and see if we can meet at least around market rate."

**Cosmin's rewrite:** "I want to jump on a call this week to talk about my scope and future inside the company. My scope and hours have grown, and I want to discuss a new structure that motivates me more and helps you make more — let me know when you've got time."

**The lesson:** when you renegotiate with an existing client, you have the leverage. Drop the permission-asking and the vague "market rate," and name the call's purpose with confidence.

### Worked Example — How a Coach Approaches a New Prospect

A senior's process on a new supplement brand complaining of "creative fatigue": first, find what makes the product genuinely unique by running the offer through a reverse-brief. Then test market archetypes, not just features — the high-performance optimizer, the quietly-declining professional, the former athlete. Diagnose their current winners surgically. The reframe: "the fatigue is happening because you're only targeting one market — there are all these others you could reach."

**A junior asks "what ad should I write?" A senior asks "who is this for, where does it sit in the funnel, what's the unique mechanism, and which untested archetype is the opening?"**

## Lesson 9 — Contingency Plans & Explore × Exploit

Once you have a few clients, the game shifts from getting work to managing the portfolio — knowing which clients are for learning, which are for money, when to leave, and how to leave without fear running the decision.

### Explore versus Exploit

When you're starting out, or with a blue-chip you want for the resume, you're in explore mode — the goal isn't maximum money, because the learning and the data pay for themselves. Once you have proof, you switch to exploit — only the clients you want, paid properly.

"People with self-worth issues stay in explore mode their whole life — they're Dora the Explorer forever, and they never get anything."

**Two rules keep explore mode honest:**
1. Explore clients must give you data — no spend data means no learning, which makes it a bad explore client. Demand spend-per-ad and a numeric definition of "winner."
2. Set a concrete graduation metric so you don't stay for comfort years past the point of value.

### The Contingency Plan: An If-Then Document

For each client, write if-then statements — "once I hit this level of winner, I'll either raise the rate (to this) or replace them (with this kind of client). This is what would make me leave, this is how I'd renegotiate." It should fit on one page.

**Supporting moves:**
- **Engineer the number to the penny.** Luke refuses ranges: "give me the roadmap to $25k — which client pays what, which one I drop, which I raise."
- **Rank clients by dollars-per-ad and let the math cut.** Don't keep easy money if it blocks better money or drifts your scope.
- **Firing is a business decision.** "Are you allowed to change your mind? Are you fulfilling what they pay for? Then there's no moral issue — and they'd replace you in a heartbeat."
- **"Let's test it" dissolves creative-control fights:** "I'll write the ads you want and put a little spend on mine. If mine crush, great; if not, I'll shut up."

The replacement fear is almost always a story. One member: "to think — when I dropped my long-time client I worried it'd take forever to replace them, lol."

## Lesson 10 — Copy Rules + Hooks

### The Eight Copy Rules

1. **3rd–5th grade reading level.** Not subjective — run it through the Hemingway app. "I've almost never seen copy get worse by lowering the reading level."
2. **Clean out all AI-isms and weird staccato pacing.**
3. **Hooks must be vicious.** "Holy shit" is the standard. Nothing underneath it counts.
4. **Know all the CopyBlocks cold** — pain, promise, proof, constraints, curiosity, conditions — and use them in every ad.
5. **Make every block as strong as possible** (clear, relevant, emotional, specific).
6. **One idea, one clear identity per ad.** You should be able to name it — "the Ice Cube ad." If you can't, it isn't one idea yet.
7. **Remove all fluff and throat-clearing.** "Start the ad where the ad starts — if it's an ice-cube ad and the ice cube isn't in line one, cut that."
8. **Curiosity over pain.** "Don't open with 'it's 2:17am, you're staring at the ceiling.' Nobody wants to read that — it's AI slop."

**The copy stew (one idea per ad):** People use multiple ideas because they don't have confidence in their one idea, so they pile on. Fine dining is simple, precise ingredients. Vague copy is a thinking problem, not a wording problem — don't fix the sentence, finish the thought.

**Vicious doesn't mean non-compliant.** You can write a branded, compliant hook that's still vicious. When a client pushes back on your hook: "test it both ways. If yours crushes theirs, they'll stop pushing."

**Hooks first, always.** Write the hooks before the body, because when you start vicious the whole ad stays vicious; start with a vanilla body and your hooks come out soft. "Hooks are 99% of creative strategy now — the body can be mediocre if the hook is fire."

### Worked Thread — Should I Invent a New Mechanism?

Cosmin's answer: "My approach is to do more of what's already working while taking a few big swings — but I rarely create new mechanisms. That's the fastest way to get winners. Frame the existing mechanism as the reason the competitors' solutions fail, and why yours is the only one that works. Then take big swings."

**Takeaway:** characterize the villain, don't reflexively invent new mechanisms, and use fresh research to explain why competitors fail rather than to build a brand-new contraption.

## Lesson 11 — What's Working Right Now

### The Realist Baseline

Most ads fail — somewhere between 90 and 95% won't work, so a 10–20% strike rate is genuinely excellent. Obsessive polishing of any single ad is mathematically a bad bet. Winners are often "disjointed" and still crush, so don't agonize over every word. You are a bad judge of your own ads — let the market decide.

### The Andromeda Era (Notes from a Real Operator)

From a founder running a ~$75M brand chasing $250M:

- **The junior-to-senior gap is enormous.** A junior writes copy and rewrites formats; a senior thinks about who the message is for, where it sits in the funnel, and ties the writing to live spend signal.
- **The platform has shifted.** Three years ago the winners were long-form advertorials and VSLs with tight congruence. Today the big spenders run broad funnels — quiz funnels, listicles, direct-to-product-page — that can answer multiple personas in one place, because the algorithm now does the targeting at the ad layer.
- **Hooks are dead; net-new ads are in.** Running five hooks off one video no longer works. What works is net-new ads with very different leads and visually different avatars, and then taking a winning message and translating it into entirely different formats.
- **Performance over volume.** Each strategist owns a revenue target. They killed win-rate metrics because those reward ads that fizzle after a day. "If one ad takes 60% of the month's spend, you've done your job."
- **What makes a great strategist: obsession.** The top people consume marketing in their free time and notice trends early.
- **The pay:** juniors at $5–6k plus bonuses, top of the range at $15–17k base, and "$50k/month for the right person."

### Iterations: Turning One Winner into Ten

Once you have a winner, the job isn't to write something new — it's to milk it. On a winner, test new hooks and images; everywhere else, take big swings rather than tweaking small things. "For video I'll test five hooks and a body, otherwise I go for new concepts. For statics I almost always go big, and the main thing I test is different images, not copy. For long-form, I test images over new copy."

**Cosmin's most-recent learnings on winners:**
- **Segments are the biggest unlock.** "The copy doesn't even have to be perfect, because there's no competition." Find them in reviews, then build the angle.
- **Stack the hook and lead with as many copy blocks as possible.** "Some creatives win just because you say more, sooner. If your promise is on the third line, they've already left."
- **Know the four types of ads:** breakthrough (crushing from launch), spend winners (eating spend without hitting KPIs), KPI winners (the best), and losers.
- **Dimensionalize your outcomes and promises** — answer "so what?" over and over until every script is full of vivid, specific outcomes.

## Lesson 12 — The Overall Creative Diversity System

**Creative Diversity = Segments × Visual/Creative × Awareness Levels × Angle/Messaging**

Four pillars, weighted differently, give you the healthiest ad account. The most immediate places to put your attention: new segments, unaware angles, and new visuals.

**On the visual pillar:** the algorithm heavily weights visuals when deciding whether something is a genuinely new ad. So expand the visual palette as far as it goes — UGC, native statics, templated statics, animation, "Yapper" style, POV, even testing different avatar ages and looks.

**The grid in practice (a five-ad test that moved five different levers):**
- A senior-pet warning ad, alarm-bell tone, documentary B-roll
- A humor "POV" ad with no creator on camera
- A new-owner panic ad, single-take, no edits
- A side-by-side visual-proof ad, split-screen, no narration
- A "here's what would happen" forward-projection ad

Five ads, five genuinely different bets — or, as the principle goes, you only really made one ad.

**The thread that kept resurfacing all cohort: segments** — the highest-leverage, least-used lever. Find new segments and sub-segments in reviews and competitor gaps.

## Lesson 13 — Hooks & Ideas

Ideation is the foundational skill. Running out of ideas is really running out of input, not talent.

### The Order: Idea, Then Hook, Then Script

One member's speed breakthrough: they used to write the ad and bolt hooks on top. Switching to idea → hook → script let them generate fifty ideas in two hours, pick nine, and have nine ads' worth of hooks and overlays ready in that same window.

### The Hook-Generation System

Build yourself a document structured as: instructions, then examples, then guidelines. The key move is **pre-hook ideation**, done before you generate anything:

1. Read the winning ad and pull out every interesting idea, standout line, and mechanism
2. For each seed, ask: how could I promote this into the main hook?
3. **Transfers:** if the hook uses coffee, coffee is a ritual — what other rituals could replace it?
4. **Reframes:** "it's not X, it's Y" — for example, "it's not low T, it's high cortisol."
5. **Promotions:** take a line buried in the body and make it the opening.

**Good hooks are tangible** — specific symptoms, patterns, numbers, something concrete. You only get roughly 10–20 words before the fold. Then multiply with AI: drop your primer and your ads into a couple of bot chats, ask for ten hooks each, and tell it to make them better.

### Where Ideas Come From

Ideation is a research problem. When you stall after one or two concepts, you've run out of input — the fix is more raw research, not more thinking.

- **Use deep research, Reddit, Quora, and adjacent ads.**
- **Steal, then feed examples to AI.** One member screenshotted a peer's winning ideas, pasted them in, and asked for thirty more like them — and got gold. "The best thing you can do with AI is plug in examples." The one guardrail: don't rip inside your own vertical.
- **Build bots that write like you.** Train a bot on your own writing and examples for a specific offer — but write the first ten or twelve ads by hand first, so the bot has something real to learn from.
- **Prime your brain before you touch AI.** Read all the research and get into the customer's deepest fears, then brain-dump twenty of your own ideas before letting a bot write the last ten.
- **Build a swipe habit.** Fifteen minutes a day studying winning ads and adjacent markets.

## Lesson 14 — Writing Faster

Speed was the single biggest skill unlock in the cohort. Faster usually means better, because speed kills the obsessive over-polishing where quality actually goes to die.

- **Give yourself less time on purpose.** "To do more in less time, give yourself less time, and you'll figure it out."
- **The mechanic: a hard quota, a hard timer, and no touch-ups.** "Pick a number of ads and a time challenge — after that, you don't touch them up, they're done."
- **The 90-minute test.** To a member who spent four hours and felt it needed ten: "did you set a 90-minute timer? I'd bet the quality wouldn't have been much different at all."
- **Track your hours.** One member discovered only 20% of their "work time" was actual ad work.
- **The 75-to-90% tax.** The output's 75% okay, but getting it to 90% eats the day. Know where to stop.
- **Bring drafts at the asked-for altitude, not gold-plated.** "Feedback at 75% is instant; grinding to 90% solo costs ten hours."
- **Body copy should take about ten minutes.** Reallocate saved time to hooks and ideation.
- **Match the tool to the voice.** One member couldn't get good copy from a DR-tuned bot because it was too direct-response, and switched to a Claude project that fit the brand's softer style.
- **Never trade away the feedback loop.** "The faster I go, the more I sacrifice quality — and I've been requesting less feedback because I'm moving so fast. That's a mistake." Go fast and over-submit.

## Lesson 15 — Video Briefs & Working With Editors

A huge amount of a strategist's leverage is in the handoff to editors.

### Keep Briefs Simple by Default

Cosmin's whole philosophy is "copy plus guidelines." Most of his briefs are short-form scripts — five hooks, a video guideline or two, and a reference. Match the brief's detail to the editor and the client.

### The Editor-Direction Checklist

When an edit comes back wrong, this is the level of specificity that fixes it:
- **Voiceover:** match the accent to the market; needs emotional range and emphasis on key phrases. Study the winning ad's cadence and replicate it.
- **Captions:** remove the highlight that follows each word, restyle to look natural like the winner, sync the caption to what's being said right now.
- **Music:** don't start it at the very top — bring it in once the story starts.
- **Hook pacing:** the hook is the most important part, so emotional inflection matters most.

**Always find the swipe first.** The hardest mode is writing with no reference ad at all.

### Ryan's Editor Process and the Numbers

Script, plus a Loom, plus an example of how you want it to look — then a feedback loop in Frame.io (timestamped comments beat Dropbox). Over-invest in feedback early to train the editor up.

- Full-time editor: ~$2k/month for roughly 20–30 scripts
- For UGC and "Yapper" creators: sometimes full script, sometimes just an outline — depends on how good the creator is

### The Systematized AI Brief

One member built an AI brief generator on top of a master swipe-file library — each winning ad logged with its transcript, a folder of reference clips, and a manual breakdown of why each hook works. The workflow: paste in a new script, and the system finds the closest references from the library, then goes line by line through the script with a visual recommendation for each line. "What makes or breaks it is the swipe library — you could even build one per client."

## Lesson 16 — Resources & Advertorials

Advertorials are becoming as important as ads. A single broad advertorial can answer multiple personas in one place.

### Advertorial Fundamentals

- **Pricing reference:** a floor around $1,000 per advertorial, with scaling bonuses on top
- **Time expectation:** first attempts ran anywhere from a couple of hours to five-plus
- **Destination logic:** an advertorial or third-party listicle is not the product page — compliance rules generally relax on third-party pages
- **Use it as a diagnostic:** when click-through on the page tanks, a new advertorial is often the fix
- **The skill spec:** choose one clear identity, one good idea, make the headlines and subheads excellent, edit the language to be readable yet still read like genuine editorial

### The Tool Stack

- **Hemingway** — reading level

- **Claude projects** — softer brand-voice work

## Lesson 17 — The Importance of Mentorship

Every member's biggest wins came partly from the cohort — peers feeding leads, coaches reading their track record back to them, the simple fact of being held accountable.

### Use the Room You Paid For

Ryan: "I've been surprised at the lack of submissions — you paid for this, use us." The frame that should change how you show up is the alley-oop: "we're giving you an alley-oop to achieve exactly what you want. We can only give it to you — it's up to you to make it happen. You'll never get an easier opportunity. All you have to do is be your word."

### Reputation Cuts Both Ways

Inside a good room, your reputation is a referral engine — or an anti-referral engine. Luke knows talented people missing six figures of work he'd happily refer them, except they can't be trusted to keep their word.

### Agencies Are the Track-Record Cheat Code

Cosmin's own path went from $2,000/month at an agency to multiples of that, because the agency gave him cheap reps on big brands with real ad-spend data — and once his ads were behind serious spend, everything got easier. Agencies pay poorly but hand you the proof you can't get anywhere else fast. Treat them as explore clients, and raise the rate once you're a catch.

### Feedback Is the Accelerant — If You Learn, Not Patch

The growth came from a tight loop: write daily, get feedback, outreach, repeat. The meta-skill is coachability — "new strategists are so hard to coach because they don't take the feedback and learn from it; they just fix the one piece of copy." Submit first to be first in the queue, and extract the principle, not the patch.

### Peers: Proof Machines, Referral Network, and Mirror

Members bootstrapped each other's testimonials and reviews, passed each other real leads, and cross-pollinated constantly. A blind spot, by definition, is the thing you can't see yourself.

### What You're Really Buying Is Accountability

"I've gotten so much more done because I'm being held accountable — I normally procrastinate." The skill is teachable anywhere; what you're really buying is the room, the standard, and the people who won't let you off the hook.
